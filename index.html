    Off the Clock

    Repairs, teardowns and builds I've taken on outside of work. Tap one to see how it went.

    • Learning electronics by building (and breaking)

      It started with one screen saying hello, and turned into a habit of opening things up to see how they work, including one lesson about heat I learned the hard way.

      1. Hello, world. My first real Arduino circuit. Wired it up, flashed it, and the screen said hello back.
      2. The dead drive. I hooked an old hard drive up to my laptop to recover its data. It wouldn't power on, and the problem looked like the board itself.
      3. The homemade soldering iron. Fixing that board meant soldering, and I didn't own an iron, so I built one: pencil lead, an old phone charger and wrapped wire. It got hot. So did my fingers.
      4. Why did it stop? Our dehumidifier died, so I took it apart to find out how it actually works. A crash course in Peltier cooling and thermodynamics.
      5. Just for fun. Bored one day, I gutted an old laptop nobody at home was using anymore and gave myself one goal: get the smallest operating system I could find running on it. No reason. Just curiosity.
      6. The idea. College group project: pick an idea and build it. Ours was a toilet lid that opens on its own when you walk up.
      7. The prototype. Our working prototype: walk up and the lid lifts, over a bowl we 3D printed.

    • Bringing a MacBook back from the dead

      It started with a dead memory slot on my 2011 MacBook Pro's board. Rather than settle for a repair, I put a 2012 logic board in the 2011 body, something it was never built to take, shaved down a connector to make it fit and tracked down why it wouldn't boot. It came back as a 2012, faster graphics and memory included, now running a macOS Apple never released for it.

      1. Gutted. Everything out. The hard drive became an SSD and the battery was replaced.
      2. Fresh paste. Thermal paste is what carries heat off the processor. Years old, it had dried out, and a chip that can't shed heat runs hot and slows itself down. I took the board out, cleaned the old paste off and put fresh paste on.
      3. Making it fit. The 2012 board was never meant to go in a 2011 body, and its display connector simply didn't fit. So I literally took a sheet of sandpaper and filed the connector's edges down by hand, a little at a time. File too far and I'd wear through the insulation and short the board, or crack the fragile connector for good. It clicked in.
      4. Stuck at boot. First attempt: it powered on but wouldn't boot properly, so it was back to troubleshooting to track down the fault.
      5. It boots. With the fault fixed, it came up as a 2012. Then I went one step further and unofficially installed macOS Sonoma, a version Apple never supported on this model. One less laptop in a landfill.

    • Working on my own car

      A 2007 Honda Civic closing in on 200,000 miles, and a habit of trying the fix myself before calling in a mechanic. Every repair started the same way: a symptom, hours of research and tutorials, then doing it myself until it worked.

      1. AC out, mid summer. The AC quit in the middle of summer. Ninety degree heat, a low pressure refrigerant gauge and an electrical check on the compressor clutch, working through it one tutorial at a time.
      2. First engine work. My first time working inside an engine. This is what a spark plug looks like after almost 200,000 miles.
      3. Running rough. The engine was running rough. Hours of troubleshooting, well past midnight, came down to a dirty throttle body. One careful cleaning later, it was running noticeably better.
      4. What 180,000 miles leaves behind. The transmission had never had its fluid changed. Grinding, violent shaking, jerky shifts and a transmission code. When the drain plug came out, its magnet was coated in burnt black sludge: raw metal shavings from inside the transmission.
      5. Fresh fluid. After weeks of research, a drain and fill with fresh fluid. It shifted buttery smooth again, and I saved about $2,000.
